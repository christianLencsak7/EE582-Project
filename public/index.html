      A QUICK REFRESHER

      Read the signals, then compare.

      Channels & overlap

      A Wi-Fi channel is a numbered portion of radio spectrum. Nearby channel numbers can use some of the same frequencies: that is overlap. For our 2.4 GHz, 20 MHz demonstration, we compare the common separated channel plan of 1, 6, and 11. Cisco: channel planning.

      Our overlap weights are a teaching choice, not a physical spectral mask.

      RSSI, dBm & signal strength

      RSSI stands for received signal strength indicator. Radios can report it differently; this lab represents received power in dBm, decibels relative to 1 milliwatt. Cisco: RSSI and logarithmic power.

      −40 dBm is 30 dB above −70 dBm: 1,000 times the power. dBm is logarithmic, so we convert to linear power before adding contributions. Adding dBm numbers directly would not add their powers. Fortinet: power scale.

      What does the score mean?

      Our model adds each neighbor's relative power multiplied by its overlap weight. A lower score means less weighted neighboring power in this model, not a faster measured connection.

      Counting networks alone is misleading: on the same channel, one −40 dBm neighbor contributes 10 units, while three −60 dBm neighbors contribute only 0.3 units combined.

      UNDER THE HOOD

      Simple rules.
      Visible reasoning.

      A transparent heuristic for learning channel selection. No AI model runs inside this tool.

      01

      Weight the received power

      Convert dBm to linear relative power: 10^((RSSI + 50) / 10). A −50 dBm signal has weight 1. A signal 10 dB stronger has ten times the weight.

      02

      Account for channel overlap

      max(0, 1 − |candidate − neighbor| / 5). Nearby channels contribute more. This triangular function is a project assumption, not an IEEE spectral mask.

      03

      Compare channels 1, 6 and 11

      Sum power × overlap for each candidate. Choose a minimum; retain your current channel if it ties. Otherwise, the lowest-numbered minimum breaks the tie.

      WORKED EXAMPLE / CHANNEL OVERLAP PRESET

      One neighbor. Three different scores.

      Place one neighbor on channel 3 at −50 dBm. Use “Channel overlap” above to reproduce this example.

      1. Convert power: 10^((−50 + 50) / 10) = 1 relative unit.
      2. Calculate distance and overlap: distances from channel 3 to candidates 1, 6, and 11 are 2, 3, and 8. Apply max(0, 1 − distance / 5).
      3. Multiply and sum: there is only one neighbor, so each score is 1 × overlap.
      Exact results for the project's triangular heuristic
      CandidateOverlap weightScore
      Channel 11 − 2/5 = 0.61 × 0.6 = 0.6
      Channel 61 − 3/5 = 0.41 × 0.4 = 0.4
      Channel 11max(0, 1 − 8/5) = 01 × 0 = 0

      Starting on channel 1 and applying channel 11 saves a comparison of 0.6 → 0: a reduction of 0.6 relative units, or 100% model score reduction. This does not mean a 100% Wi-Fi speed improvement or prove that the real channel is interference-free.

      A model for learning, not a site survey

      Scores use relative units. They do not predict Mbps, latency, channel utilization, collision probability, or measured interference. Real co-channel Wi-Fi can share airtime through contention (Cisco: CSMA/CA). This model does not simulate CSMA/CA, traffic load, hidden nodes, walls, multipath, or non-Wi-Fi interferers. It assumes channels 1–11, fixed 20 MHz operation, and synthetic neighbors at one observation point. It does not scan Wi-Fi or change router settings. AI assists development; a deterministic formula makes the recommendations.
